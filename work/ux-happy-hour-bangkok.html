UX Happy Hour Bangkok · Community building

Where Bangkok's designers finally found each other.

Role: Founder & organiser  ·  Years: 2015–2025  ·  Duration: 10 years  ·  Domain: Community building

Context

When I moved to Bangkok from Chicago in 2015, I expected to plug into a vibrant UX community. Instead I found a thriving tech scene with almost no UX-specific infrastructure — occasional talks, little networking, and designers working in isolation from one another. There was a growing population of practitioners and no regular place for them to connect. Having co-organised UX Happy Hour Chicago, I knew the format and what made it work.

The approach

I treated the community as a product. The first event drew ten people — mostly entrepreneurs, not designers — and instead of calling that a failure, I treated it as user research: the conversations revealed the specific online channels where Bangkok's designers actually gathered. Event two pivoted the venue from a hotel bar to affordable coffee shops with group seating and used those channels; turnout jumped. Event three surfaced a cultural dynamic — attendees were hesitant to approach strangers — so I introduced structured individual introductions and guided group discussions to build psychological safety.

What made it work

Four deliberate design decisions drove its longevity: quality over scale (word-of-mouth, not mass advertising — a community people brought friends to, not a list of names); cultural UX (adapting Western networking formats to local social norms); consistency over intensity (monthly without exception, so the community became a trust signal people could rely on); and a co-organiser model that brought in complementary networks and removed single-point-of-failure dependency.

Outcome

UX Happy Hour Bangkok grew to 1,700+ members and ran monthly for ten years, becoming one of Southeast Asia's longest-running design communities before coming to a planned finale in 2025.

What it proves

Building something from zero to 1,700+ over ten years — without budget, without employer backing, by adapting the product to its cultural context — draws on the same muscles as building a design organisation: user empathy, iteration, systems thinking, and sustained commitment to something that matters.
